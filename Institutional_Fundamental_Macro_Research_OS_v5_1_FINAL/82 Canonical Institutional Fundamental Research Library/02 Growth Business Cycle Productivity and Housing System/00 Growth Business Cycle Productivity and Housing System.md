## 7. Regime and multihorizon logic

- **Structural:** legal architecture, demographics, technology, resource base, long-run balance sheets and institutional credibility.
- **Cyclical:** growth, inflation, credit, profits, policy and financing over quarters.
- **Tactical:** expectations, valuation, issuance, positioning and policy repricing over weeks.
- **Multi-day:** persistent new information, catalyst path, carry, funding and forced-flow risk.
- **Day/event:** surprise vector, immediate inference, information half-life and cross-market confirmation.

A lower-horizon shock can change timing, distribution and confidence without automatically reversing the structural state. The conclusion must be reported separately by horizon.

## 15. Deep macro-system interpretation

Macro analysis begins with reconciliation rather than narrative. Production, income and expenditure must describe the same economy, while sectoral balances determine which sector is accumulating financial claims and which is issuing liabilities. A growth acceleration financed by household dissaving, bank credit or fiscal transfers has a different durability and asset transmission from one financed by productivity, export income or retained corporate earnings. The analyst therefore reconstructs both the expenditure impulse and the balance-sheet carrier of that impulse.

Turning points are inferred from a hierarchy. Hard activity data establish realized production and demand; labor, income and tax receipts reveal breadth; surveys and orders provide earlier but noisier information; credit, inventories and housing expose financing and duration sensitivity. Revisions matter because initial releases systematically omit information and can alter the apparent sequence. A current-state report must say whether the conclusion is based on observed levels, diffusion, acceleration, revision, forecast or judgment.

Inflation and growth are not independent labels. The composition of demand, supply elasticity, wage/productivity dynamics, import prices, markups, housing lags and fiscal incidence determine whether nominal growth improves real income or merely raises the price level. The relevant policy reaction is endogenous: the same growth print may support risky assets in a benign disinflation regime and damage them when it raises the expected policy path or term premium.

The canonical synthesis separates four objects: real state, nominal-income state, distribution across sectors, and the market's prior. Scenario work must show which balance-sheet sector absorbs the adjustment. If households retrench, does government dissaving offset it? If banks restrict credit, can capital markets substitute? If imports compress, is the improvement a competitiveness gain or domestic-demand weakness? This accounting discipline prevents stories that cannot coexist.
