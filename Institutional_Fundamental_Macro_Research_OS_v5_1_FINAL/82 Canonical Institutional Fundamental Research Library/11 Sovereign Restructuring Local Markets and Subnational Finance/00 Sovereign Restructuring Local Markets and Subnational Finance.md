## 7. Regime and multihorizon logic

- **Structural:** legal architecture, demographics, technology, resource base, long-run balance sheets and institutional credibility.
- **Cyclical:** growth, inflation, credit, profits, policy and financing over quarters.
- **Tactical:** expectations, valuation, issuance, positioning and policy repricing over weeks.
- **Multi-day:** persistent new information, catalyst path, carry, funding and forced-flow risk.
- **Day/event:** surprise vector, immediate inference, information half-life and cross-market confirmation.

A lower-horizon shock can change timing, distribution and confidence without automatically reversing the structural state. The conclusion must be reported separately by horizon.

## 11. Falsification, uncertainty and failure modes

- Reject conclusions that depend on an unidentified residual being treated as a structural factor.
- Cap confidence when measurement, sampling, revision or liquidity uncertainty is material.
- Separate absence of evidence from evidence of absence.
- Record the strongest contrary official or market evidence.
- State the expected sign and timing of leading indicators before observing the target outcome.
- Distinguish a wrong state estimate, wrong priced baseline, wrong causal model, wrong horizon and exogenous residual.

## 15. Deep external-balance and currency interpretation

A currency is the clearing price of several balance sheets. Trade in goods and services, primary income, portfolio flows, direct investment, bank funding, derivatives hedging, reserve management and valuation changes all matter. The balance of payments is an accounting identity, but the exchange-rate adjustment depends on price elasticity, currency denomination, maturity, hedging, investor home bias and the credibility of the policy regime.

Global-dollar analysis distinguishes dollar credit created by U.S. banks, non-U.S. banks, bond markets and FX swaps. Covered-interest-parity deviations are balance-sheet prices, not free arbitrage in the presence of capital, liquidity, credit, margin and regulatory constraints. Cross-currency basis can reflect hedging demand or intermediary scarcity and must be read with secured funding, quarter-end effects and collateral conditions.

Currency valuation models—PPP, BEER, FEER, carry, momentum and external-balance frameworks—answer different questions and operate at different horizons. A cheap currency can remain weak under deteriorating terms of trade or credibility; a high-yield currency can appreciate until funding liquidity, reserve adequacy or political risk changes discontinuously. Intervention analysis separates sterilized spot operations, forward books, reserve-optionality, capital controls and communication.

For emerging markets, the analyst maps foreign-currency debt, local-market foreign ownership, reserve liquidity, current-account financing, bank and corporate mismatches, fiscal dominance and domestic institutional demand. A historical reconstruction must use reserve data and policy measures available at the cutoff rather than later crisis labels.
