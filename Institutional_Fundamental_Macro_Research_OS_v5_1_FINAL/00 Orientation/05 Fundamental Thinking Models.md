## Preserved subject-specific foundation

This material survived consolidation because it contains subject-specific instruction for **Fundamental Thinking Models**, not the former repeated institutional wrapper.

## 1. State–Expectation–Price Model

- **State:** the economy and financial system as they are.
- **Expectation:** what participants believe the future state will be.
- **Price:** the discounted and risk-adjusted expression of those expectations.
- **Return:** the result of expectation change plus carry, income, and flow effects.

Use this model before every interpretation.

## 2. Reaction-Function Model

Do not ask only, “Is inflation high?” Ask:

- What does the central bank target?
- Which variable has the highest policy weight now?
- How asymmetric is the response?
- What constraints exist?
- How credible is communication?
- How much of the response is priced?

## 3. Constraint Model

Large players act under constraints:

- benchmark tracking,
- risk limits,
- collateral and margin,
- regulatory capital,
- duration mandates,
- hedging requirements,
- redemption pressure,
- end-of-period rebalancing,
- policy mandates.

A constraint can dominate a discretionary view.

## 4. Marginal Buyer/Seller Model

Prices are set at the margin. Ask:

- Who must transact today?
- Who is price-sensitive?
- Who is not price-sensitive?
- Who is leveraged?
- Who is short gamma?
- Who must rebalance after a large move?
- Which participant controls the session?

## 5. Balance-Sheet Model

Every asset move affects balance sheets, which can amplify the next move.

- Higher collateral values can enable more leverage.
- Lower asset prices can trigger margin calls.
- A stronger dollar can tighten global dollar balance sheets.
- Wider credit spreads can reduce risk capacity.
- Falling volatility can invite systematic leverage.

## 6. Distribution Model

A consensus forecast is not a complete expectation. Consider:

- distribution width,
- skew,
- whisper estimates,
- option-implied event range,
- prior revisions,
- component expectations,
- and positioning.

A number close to consensus can still cause a large move when the distribution was asymmetric.

## 7. Dominant-Constraint Model

At any moment, one constraint often dominates:

- inflation constraint,
- growth constraint,
- funding constraint,
- fiscal supply constraint,
- earnings constraint,
- geopolitical constraint,
- or positioning constraint.

Find the dominant constraint before ranking indicators.

## 8. Causal-Chain Model

Write the chain explicitly:

> Information → expectation revision → policy/rate/cash-flow change → risk-premium or flow response → asset impact → cross-asset confirmation → price acceptance.

If the chain has missing links, confidence should fall.

## 9. Counterfactual Model

Ask:

- What would the market have done if the data were identical but positioning were neutral?
- What if yields did not move?
- What if credit spreads remained calm?
- What if the central bank were in a different regime?
- What observation would distinguish my explanation from a competing explanation?

## 10. Asymmetry Model

The best context is not merely likely; it is asymmetric.

Asymmetry can come from:

- a large gap between pricing and plausible outcomes;
- one-sided positioning;
- a central-bank reaction-function kink;
- an underestimated tail;
- a catalyst with limited downside but large upside;
- or a cross-asset divergence that cannot persist.
