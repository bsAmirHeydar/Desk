## Three Engines of Asset Prices

For risk assets, most fundamental movement can be organized into:

1. expected cash flows;
2. discount rates;
3. risk premia.

### Expected Cash Flows

For equities:

- revenue growth;
- margins;
- earnings;
- buybacks;
- dividends;
- default survival.

For commodities:

- expected scarcity and convenience yield;
- storage and carrying economics;
- future supply and demand.

For currencies:

- expected relative return and external demand for the currency.

### Discount Rates

The present value of future cash flows falls when the discount rate rises.

Discount rates contain:

- expected risk-free rates;
- inflation compensation;
- term premium;
- credit spread;
- equity risk premium;
- currency risk premium.

Long-duration assets such as high-growth technology equities are generally more sensitive to changes in real yields and terminal discount rates because more of their expected value lies further in the future.

### Risk Premia

Risk premium is the compensation investors demand for holding uncertainty.

It changes with:

- volatility;
- leverage;
- liquidity;
- recession probability;
- funding stress;
- geopolitical risk;
- policy credibility;
- market depth;
- and portfolio constraints.

## Why Yields Can Rise for Different Reasons

A rise in the ten-year yield can reflect:

- higher expected policy rates;
- higher inflation compensation;
- higher real growth expectations;
- higher term premium;
- more Treasury supply;
- lower demand for duration;
- or fiscal credibility concerns.

These are not equivalent.

### Growth-led yield rise

Potentially constructive for equities if earnings revisions dominate.

### Real-yield or policy-led rise

Potentially negative for long-duration equities and gold.

### Term-premium or fiscal-supply-led rise

Can tighten financial conditions even without stronger growth.

### Inflation-breakeven-led rise

Can help nominally sensitive assets at first, but damage them if policy credibility deteriorates.

## A Practical Equity Decomposition

When Nasdaq moves, ask:

1. Did expected earnings change?
2. Did real yields change?
3. Did policy expectations change?
4. Did credit conditions change?
5. Did the equity risk premium change?
6. Did mega-cap-specific news dominate?
7. Did positioning or options flow amplify the move?

## Bad News / Good News Regimes

“Bad news is good news” typically appears when:

- inflation is the dominant constraint;
- weaker activity lowers expected policy rates;
- credit stress remains contained;
- earnings damage is not yet severe.

“Bad news is bad news” appears when:

- growth deterioration threatens cash flows;
- credit spreads widen;
- policy easing is seen as insufficient or reactive;
- financial instability dominates.

The transition between these regimes is critical.

## Cross-Asset Test

A rate-relief equity rally is stronger when:

- front-end yields fall;
- real yields fall;
- the dollar softens;
- credit spreads remain stable or tighten;
- volatility falls;
- breadth improves.

A rally with falling yields but widening credit spreads may be a temporary duration reaction inside a deeper growth scare.

## Intraday Use

Do not read index price alone. Build a compact monitor:

- two-year Treasury yield;
- ten-year real yield or TIPS proxy;
- dollar index;
- credit ETF or spread proxy;
- VIX and short-dated volatility;
- equal-weight vs cap-weight index;
- semiconductors vs defensives;
- oil and gold where relevant.

The purpose is causal diagnosis, not indicator accumulation.
