## What It Is

Commodity prices reflect physical supply-demand balance, inventories, storage, transportation, quality/location constraints, expected future balance, financing, currencies, and risk premia. Financial flows matter, but physical constraints anchor medium-term behavior.

## Why Markets Care

The market does not trade the variable in isolation. It trades how the variable changes expected policy, cash flows, default risk, risk premia, and relative returns.

## Driver Map

- Global and regional demand.
- Producer capacity, outages, and spare capacity.
- OPEC+ or producer policy.
- Inventories and storage capacity.
- Seasonality, weather, and refinery runs.
- Freight and geographic dislocation.
- Forward-curve structure and convenience yield.
- Currency and financing conditions.
- Geopolitical risk and sanctions.

## Indicator Hierarchy

### Leading and High-Frequency

- Producer announcements and outage data.
- Shipping/freight and physical differentials.
- Forward curve and calendar spreads.
- Rig counts and capex.
- Weather forecasts.
- Demand nowcasts and mobility/industrial indicators.

### Coincident

- EIA inventory, production, imports, exports, and product data.
- OPEC/IEA/EIA balances.
- Refinery utilization and crack spreads.
- Spot and physical premiums.

### Lagging or Confirming

- Long-cycle supply investment.
- Demand destruction and substitution.
- Official annual reserves.

### Market-Implied or Financial

- Backwardation/contango.
- Crack spreads and product inventories.
- Commodity currencies.
- Energy equities versus spot.
- Dollar and global growth proxies.

## Transmission to Markets

- Tighter balances and inventory draws support prices.
- Backwardation often signals higher value of immediate supply, but can include risk premium.
- Stronger growth supports demand; stronger dollar can weigh on dollar-priced commodities.
- Oil shocks can raise inflation and reduce real income.
- Financial liquidation can temporarily dominate physical tightness.
- Different commodities have different storage and supply elasticities.

## Day-Trading Translation

For oil day trading, do not reduce the process to the headline inventory change. Decompose crude, gasoline, distillates, production, imports, exports, refinery runs, and the expected seasonal pattern. Watch prompt spreads and product cracks. For gold, use 03 Gold because its monetary and reserve-asset behavior differs from industrial commodities.

The daily objective is not to forecast the next data print from scratch. It is to know which outcome would force the largest repricing and which cross-asset market should confirm first.

## Short-Swing Translation

For swings, maintain a balance sheet: supply, demand, inventory trajectory, spare capacity, curve, physical differentials, and geopolitical probability. Identify what the market curve already discounts.

A swing thesis should survive normal intraday noise. It needs a multi-session pricing gap, a catalyst sequence, and a clearly separate overnight invalidation.

## Common Traps

- Using one inventory number without expectations or seasonality.
- Ignoring products and refinery utilization.
- Treating futures positioning as physical demand.
- Assuming backwardation guarantees price gains.
- Confusing announced production cuts with delivered cuts.
- Ignoring location and quality constraints.
- Using geopolitics without probability-weighted supply impact.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Is the physical balance tightening or loosening?
- Are inventories high or low versus seasonal norms?
- What does the curve imply?
- Are product markets confirming crude?
- Is the move physical or financial?
- What supply is genuinely at risk?
- How elastic are supply and demand over the trade horizon?
