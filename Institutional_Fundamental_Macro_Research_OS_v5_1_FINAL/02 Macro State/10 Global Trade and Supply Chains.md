## What It Is

Global trade and supply chains transmit demand, production, shipping, inventory, currency, and geopolitical shocks across economies. They affect growth, goods inflation, margins, and commodity demand.

## Why Markets Care

The market does not trade the variable in isolation. It trades how the variable changes expected policy, cash flows, default risk, risk premia, and relative returns.

## Driver Map

- Final demand and inventory cycles.
- Manufacturing new orders and export orders.
- Shipping capacity, ports, and freight costs.
- Supplier delivery times and bottlenecks.
- Trade policy, tariffs, sanctions, and export controls.
- Currency movements.
- Energy and commodity availability.
- Geographic concentration and substitution capacity.

## Indicator Hierarchy

### Leading and High-Frequency

- Export orders and manufacturing PMIs.
- Freight rates and delivery times.
- Port congestion and air-freight costs.
- Semiconductor and industrial order books.
- Trade-policy announcements.

### Coincident

- Trade volumes and customs data.
- Industrial production.
- Inventory-to-sales ratios.
- Import/export prices.

### Lagging or Confirming

- Consumer-price pass-through.
- Capacity relocation and capital expenditure.
- Productivity effects.

### Market-Implied or Financial

- Shipping and transport equities.
- Industrial metals and oil.
- Commodity currencies.
- Semiconductors and global cyclicals.
- Inflation compensation.

## Interpretation Framework

For every update, separate:

1. **Level** — where the variable stands.
2. **Momentum** — whether it is improving or deteriorating.
3. **Breadth** — how widely the change is distributed.
4. **Quality** — whether the composition is durable.
5. **Revision risk** — how much history may change.
6. **Expectation gap** — what was already priced.
7. **Policy relevance** — whether the reaction function changes.
8. **Horizon** — when the impact should appear.

## Transmission to Markets

- Supply-chain easing can reduce goods inflation and support margins.
- Disruptions can raise inflation while reducing output—a stagflationary mix.
- Trade restrictions can shift sector winners and losers.
- Inventory destocking can weaken production even when final demand is stable.
- Shipping shocks can matter for oil, industrials, FX, and rates through inflation.

## Day-Trading Translation

Use trade context when a session is exposed to China data, tariffs, shipping disruption, or semiconductor restrictions. Watch whether the shock is interpreted as inflationary, growth-negative, or both. The rates curve and oil/industrial commodities help identify the dominant channel.

The daily objective is not to forecast the next data print from scratch. It is to know which outcome would force the largest repricing and which cross-asset market should confirm first.

## Short-Swing Translation

For swings, track the inventory cycle, export orders, freight pressure, trade-policy implementation, and margin impact. A supply-chain thesis often unfolds across several data releases rather than one event.

A swing thesis should survive normal intraday noise. It needs a multi-session pricing gap, a catalyst sequence, and a clearly separate overnight invalidation.

## Common Traps

- Treating longer delivery times as always stronger demand.
- Confusing nominal trade values with real volumes.
- Ignoring inventory cycles.
- Assuming every tariff strengthens the domestic currency.
- Using shipping rates without route and contract context.
- Treating announcements as implemented policy.
- Ignoring substitution and adaptation.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Is the shock demand-driven or supply-driven?
- Are inventories building or being depleted?
- Which sectors and countries are exposed?
- Does the shock raise inflation, reduce growth, or both?
- How fast can substitution occur?
- Are freight and commodity markets confirming?
- Is the policy operational or only rhetorical?
